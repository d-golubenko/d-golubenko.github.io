Research / Battery recycling / 2025

Acid recovery from lithium battery leachate

← Research interests · All publications

We developed a proton-selective membrane by incorporating polybenzimidazole (PBI) into a commercial perfluorosulfonic acid (PFSA) membrane. Repeated low-recovery electrodialysis tests gave proton selectivity coefficients of 770 over lithium and 606 over cobalt. In a separate batch protocol, the selected composite recovered about 80% of the acid from synthetic lithium-ion battery leachate, with current efficiencies of 92–95%.

Research by Daniil Golubenko, Dmitrii Petukhov, Raed A. Al-Juboori, Sabu Varghese, Daniel Johnson, and Nidal Hilal at New York University Abu Dhabi. Published in ACS ES&T Engineering (2025). Article and Supporting Information.

Membrane design · Results · Mechanism · Limitations · Reference

Why separate acid from battery leachate?

Acid leaching brings battery metals into solution, producing a mixture of acid and dissolved metal salts. Conventional downstream processing often involves neutralizing the residual acid to adjust pH for subsequent metal separation. This consumes base and converts acid into salts instead of recovering it for reuse. Selective acid recovery offers a route to retain that reagent value while leaving a metal-containing stream for further processing. The challenge is to favour proton transport over both lithium and divalent metal ions.

Background sources cited in our paper: Larouche et al. (2020), a review of hydrometallurgical battery recycling, and López et al. (2021), membrane treatment of metal-containing acidic wastes.

How does electrodialysis drive acid recovery?

Diffusion dialysis uses a concentration gradient as its driving force. Electrodialysis applies an electric field, providing an additional way to drive and control ion transfer. Our study investigates proton-selective electrodialysis for acid recovery. For a discussion of the two acid-recovery approaches, see Yan et al. (2022), Diffusion Dialysis or Selective Electrodialysis?, cited in the paper.

How were the PBI/PFSA membranes prepared?

We formed PBI inside a commercial cation-exchange membrane by sequential treatment with a benzenetetramine precursor and terephthalaldehyde. Precursor concentrations of 2, 3, and 4 wt% were compared. A sulfonated-polystyrene membrane was also modified to test how the underlying polymer affects the outcome.

One membrane face was exposed for 10 minutes to the benzenetetramine tetrahydrochloride solution in 0.1 M HCl. After a brief rinse, it was treated for two hours with 2 wt% terephthalaldehyde in acetone. This sequential reaction introduced a PBI-containing region within the existing membrane rather than creating a separate freestanding PBI film.

The preparation builds on our earlier work on PBI-modified cation-exchange membranes for alkali/alkaline-earth metal separation (2024). The present study asks whether this material strategy can discriminate protons from metal ions and enable acid recovery.

The sample labelled PFSA-3% provided the working compromise between selectivity and electrical resistance. The “3%” refers to the concentration of the precursor solution used during preparation; it is not a measured PBI mass fraction in the final membrane.

The choice follows from the screening results: stronger modification further suppressed metal transport, but the 4% treatment also caused a much larger increase in cell resistance. The reported additional resistance was about 280 Ω cm2 for PFSA-3% and 3,000 Ω cm2 for PFSA-4%. PFSA-3% therefore offered strong proton discrimination with a smaller electrical penalty and was carried forward to the recovery experiments.

Key results and the conditions behind them

Two experimental protocols answer different questions. Low-recovery tests assessed proton selectivity with little change in feed composition. Separate batch experiments examined how much acid could be recovered as the feed became depleted.

Proton selectivity under low-recovery conditions

The feed contained 10 mM each of HCl, LiCl, and CoCl2. A six-compartment cell operated at 1.0 mA cm−2, using approximately 5.3 cm2 of active membrane area. A 1 L feed reservoir limited changes in composition during each 60-minute test.

During repeated testing over two days, PFSA-3% reached proton selectivity coefficients of 770 relative to Li+ and 606 relative to Co2+. These are selectivity coefficients, not recovery percentages. Metal release from preconditioned membrane regions affected the early measurements; establishing steady ion fluxes could take up to 24 hours.

What drives the increase in selectivity?

The fluxes show that the gain comes primarily from suppressing metal transfer while retaining proton transfer. Comparing pristine PFSA with PFSA-3%, proton flux stayed close to 8 nmol cm−2 s−1, whereas lithium flux fell from 2.03 to 0.014 and cobalt flux from 2.58 to 0.11 in the same units. These are approximately 145-fold and 23-fold reductions in the initial screening experiment. Because these tests imposed the same current density, the result indicates that a greater share of the ion transport was carried by protons.

The sulfonated-polystyrene composites showed much smaller selectivity gains under the same screening protocol. This comparison points to the importance of the host polymer: introducing PBI produces a particularly strong proton-selective transport environment in PFSA, rather than the same outcome in every cation-exchange matrix.

The later increase in H/Co selectivity to 606 should also be read alongside the equilibration history. Proton flux and cell voltage remained relatively stable while cobalt flux decreased over repeated cycles. The paper attributes part of the early cobalt transfer to ions initially retained in unmodified membrane regions. Short tests can therefore understate the selectivity reached after this transient release subsides.

Acid recovery from synthetic battery leachate

The batch feed was 100 mL containing 10 mM HCl, 10 mM LiCl, and 3 mM each of CoCl2, MnCl2, and NiCl2. The receiving solution was 100 mL of 1 mM HCl. Ag/AgCl reference electrodes controlled a 2.5 V difference between the supporting compartments.

Across three 150-minute runs, average acid recovery was approximately 80%. The membrane was cleaned with 1 M HCl between runs. Average proton flux was about 1.7 × 10−8 mol cm−2 s−1.

The batch separation factor S(H/Me) uses the proton-to-metal concentration ratio in the receiving solution, normalized by the initial feed composition. Acid recovery subtracts the receiving solution's initial acid concentration before comparing the increase with the initial feed acid concentration; the two reservoirs had equal volumes. This avoids counting the acid already present in the receiving solution as recovered acid.

The transition-metal factor is reported for Co, Mn, and Ni together, not as three independently measured factors. Their combined average flux was 5.9 × 10−12, 4.8 × 10−13, and 3.6 × 10−13 mol cm−2 s−1 in runs 1–3, respectively. Average lithium flux ranged from 2.0 × 10−10 to 4.2 × 10−10 mol cm−2 s−1.

What do the repeated recovery runs show?

The average proton flux was nearly unchanged across the three runs, while the combined transition-metal flux decreased substantially. The rise in the transition-metal separation factor thus reflects less metal reaching the receiving stream, rather than a large increase in acid throughput. Lithium remained the more readily transported impurity, consistent with the lower H/Li factors.

The 92–95% current efficiencies show that most of the applied charge contributed to proton transfer during the selected recovery window. Together with approximately 80% acid recovery, this demonstrates that the membrane's discrimination was useful during substantial feed depletion, not only in low-recovery screening. The repeated experiments also showed no visible scaling during this 150-minute window, supporting its selection for acid recovery.

How were transport and recovery measured?

Metal concentrations were measured by ICP-MS. In the low-recovery protocol, proton concentration was determined by acid–base titration. For the batch recovery experiments, acid concentration was inferred from solution conductivity, treating the contributions of the much less abundant transported metal ions as negligible.

The Supporting Information describes separate measurements of water uptake, ion-exchange capacity, and ionic conductivity. Conductivity was measured in the sodium form using a two-electrode impedance setup in deionized water at 20–22 °C, with the cell contribution subtracted. Potentiometric transport numbers were obtained from membrane potentials across 0.1 and 0.5 M NaCl solutions, accounting for junction-potential effects. These measurements characterize the material; they do not replace mixed-feed electrodialysis tests.

What might explain the proton selectivity?

Our interpretation centres on interactions between PBI nitrogen groups and PFSA sulfonic-acid groups. Ionic pairing and hydrogen bonding can reduce the space available for hydrated metal ions while maintaining routes for proton transfer. The composites showed lower water uptake and ion-exchange capacity, together with changes in ionic-domain structure.

These property changes fit a common structural explanation. Pairing between basic PBI groups and acidic PFSA groups makes some exchange sites unavailable to mobile counterions. Fewer hydrated counterions and stronger interactions within the composite reduce water uptake and compact the ionic domains. DSC also showed less freezable water, consistent with a smaller contribution from loosely bound water in the transport environment.

A tighter environment alone does not fully explain the strong discrimination between two singly charged species, H+ and Li+. The proposed hydrogen-bond network provides a route for proton transfer while hindering the movement of hydrated metal ions. This is why the study combined structural characterization with a chemical perturbation of hydrogen bonding.

This interpretation relates to earlier studies of proton-transfer pathways built from acid–base pairs, by Ge et al. (2015), and hydrogen bonding in acid-selective membranes, by Song et al. (2021). Both are cited in our paper; their materials and test conditions differ from the PBI/PFSA system discussed here.

FTIR and Raman spectroscopy, electron microscopy, X-ray scattering, and thermal measurements were used to examine these changes. Supporting experiments included elemental analysis, solid-state NMR, membrane-potential measurements, and impedance spectroscopy.

A chemical test of the proposed mechanism

Methylating the PBI component was used to perturb hydrogen bonding. Under the low-recovery protocol, the H+/Li+ selectivity fell from 631 to 23.1, while lithium transport increased. NMR of separately prepared PBI supported the occurrence of N-methylation.

The individual fluxes clarify this change: lithium flux rose from 0.014 to 0.41 nmol cm−2 s−1, approximately 29-fold, whereas proton flux changed much less, from 8.34 to 9.48. The loss of selectivity therefore came mainly from removing an obstacle to lithium transport, rather than shutting down proton transport. In the proposed mechanism, methylation disrupts hydrogen-bond interactions while retaining positively charged PBI sites.

This observation supports a role for hydrogen bonding rather than an explanation based only on charge repulsion. It remains a mechanistic interpretation supported by several measurements, rather than direct observation of every molecular transport event.

After methylation, area-specific resistance in the lithium form dropped from 370 to 4 Ω cm2. The resistance ratios for the H+, Li+, and Co2+ forms changed from 1:8:160 to 1:1.2:4.4. Easier lithium transport accompanied the loss of proton discrimination, connecting the proposed hydrogen-bond interactions with both resistance and selectivity.

The corresponding H+/Co2+ coefficients were 77.5 before methylation and 68.3 afterward. The study treats this comparison cautiously because cobalt transport had not reached equilibrium under those test conditions; it is not a conclusive test of the methylation effect on cobalt selectivity.

The sulfonated-polystyrene composites did not show the same large selectivity gain. This comparison suggests that adding PBI alone is insufficient: interactions with the host polymer and the resulting transport environment are central to the outcome.

What is the connection to biological proton channels?

Biological proton channels provide a useful analogy for distinguishing proton transfer from the motion of other cations. The paper discusses a comparison with interacting acidic and basic groups in the HV1 channel, drawing on Dudev et al. (2015), Selectivity Mechanism of the Voltage-gated Proton Channel, HV1. This analogy helps frame the hypothesis; it does not mean that a polymer membrane reproduces a biological channel's complete structure or function.

Operating considerations

  • Acid depletion and scaling. The initial 150 minutes favoured acid recovery. As the acid was depleted, lithium transport became more prominent; beyond roughly 300 minutes, water dissociation and mixed-metal hydroxide deposits affected transport. Controlling these deposits is the main operational challenge identified in the paper.
  • Selectivity has a resistance cost. More intensive modification increased resistance. The 4% preparation was not selected for the recovery experiments, despite its high initial lithium discrimination.
