Alkylated PEI membranes for electrodialysis brine mining

← Research interests · All publications

Tuning PEI membranes for concentrated brine

Electrodialysis can transfer salts from seawater reverse-osmosis brine into a concentrated stream for further processing. Anion-exchange membranes must combine useful conductivity with controlled salt and water transport. We investigated whether changing the alkyl groups attached to polyethyleneimine (PEI) could improve this balance.

Sequential alkylation preserved a high density of charged groups while adjusting membrane hydration. In synthetic brine tests, PEI-Pr-Me reached 93.0% current efficiency, while PEI-Mix reached a chloride/sulfate selectivity coefficient of 8.7. The best membrane depended on the objective: maximizing charge utilization, favoring chloride over sulfate, or limiting water transfer.

The brine-management context is discussed in the cited study by Giacalone and colleagues. Our experiments focused on membrane performance in a controlled synthetic chloride/sulfate mixture.

Why sequential alkylation worked

We formed reinforced membranes by thermally curing branched PEI with bisphenol A diglycidyl ether (BADGE), following our earlier work on PEI/epoxy membranes. Fourteen membrane formulations were examined, including an unalkylated reference (PEI-Non), single-stage methyl, ethyl, propyl, and butyl treatments, and sequential treatments.

Longer alkyl groups reduced water uptake, but single-stage propyl and butyl treatment left too few charged sites. For example, PEI-Pr had a strong-base IEC of 0.40 mmol/g and conductivity of 0.0094 mS/cm, compared with 2.50 mmol/g and 3.93 mS/cm for PEI-Me. Suppressing hydration alone therefore did not produce an effective conducting membrane.

The sequential approach first introduced longer alkyl groups and then methylated remaining sites. PEI-Pr-Me and PEI-Bu-Me used propyl or butyl followed by methyl treatment; PEI-Mix used butyl, propyl, ethyl, and methyl in sequence. This combined control of hydration with more complete quaternization.

Water uptake refers to membranes equilibrated in deionized water. Conductivity was measured by impedance spectroscopy in deionized water at 20–22 °C; apparent transport numbers were determined from membrane potentials across 0.1/0.5 M NaCl. IEC is expressed per dry membrane mass. These properties describe separate tests from the brine experiment below.

Compared with PEI-Me, PEI-Mix retained nearly the same strong-base IEC while reducing water uptake and improving the apparent transport number, at the cost of lower conductivity. PEI-Pr-Me retained higher conductivity than Mix and achieved the best current efficiency in the brine cell. Although PEI-Pr-Me and PEI-Me both absorbed 38% water, their fractions of freezable water differed (21% and 31%), indicating that total water content alone does not capture the state of water in the membrane.

Small-angle X-ray scattering and calorimetry supported changes in the hydrated ionic domains. DFT calculations of representative functional groups helped interpret the competing effects of charge, hydration, and substituent volume. The general conductivity/selectivity tradeoff is discussed in the cited work by Kitto and Kamcev.

Ion discrimination in dilute mixed solutions

In a separate six-compartment test, the diluate contained NaCl, NaF, NaNO₃, and Na₂SO₄ at 5 mM each, with 10 mM NaHCO₃ in the concentrate. Lower water uptake generally favored transport of less strongly hydrated anions. The dependence was stronger for nitrate/chloride and chloride/fluoride than for chloride/sulfate.

The paper reports its highest dilute-solution coefficients for PEI-Pr-5d: approximately 2.2 for nitrate/chloride, 9.5 for chloride/fluoride, and 1.6 for chloride/sulfate. Its low conductivity makes these selectivity values a different optimization target from high-throughput brine concentration. Partial ion dehydration provides a physical explanation for the preference for less strongly hydrated ions; a relevant cited source is the study by Epsztein and colleagues.

Protocol effects are important when comparing these coefficients with other studies. Our work on selectivity measurement, cited in this paper, explains how the supporting electrolyte and diffusion contribute to the measured separation.

Alkylation also reduced water splitting

Current–voltage measurements combined with diluate pH monitoring showed reduced water-splitting activity after alkylation, with a stronger reduction for longer alkyl groups. The unalkylated membrane contained more accessible weak-base amines, which can catalyze water dissociation. The proposed explanation is that bulkier substituents make these groups less able to interact with and polarize water molecules.

These observations came from polarization tests, where most membranes had limiting currents around 3 mA/cm². The brine concentration experiments used a much higher salt concentration and a different operating current. The pH response therefore supports the functional-group mechanism without directly quantifying water splitting during the brine test.

How the synthetic brine experiment was run

A seven-compartment cell operated at 120 mA/cm² for 400 minutes, with recirculating flows of 160 mL/min. The diluate was 1 L of 1 M NaCl + 0.1 M Na₂SO₄; the concentrate initially held 30 mL of 1 M NaCl. The larger diluate volume helped keep its composition relatively stable while salts accumulated in the smaller concentrate. Experiments were performed at least twice.

Current efficiency was calculated from the charge-equivalent increase in chloride and sulfate amounts in the concentrate. Water transport numbers describe accompanying water transfer, while P(Cl/SO₄) normalizes the ion flux ratio by the diluate composition. These measures address different aspects of the concentration process.

PEI-Pr-Me had the highest current efficiency, indicating that a larger fraction of the supplied charge contributed to accumulation of the target salts. This depends on both membrane selectivity and the cell: electrical leakage through connecting solution paths also affects the result, so high apparent transport numbers alone do not guarantee the highest current efficiency.

PEI-Mix favored chloride most strongly, with P(Cl/SO₄) = 8.7, compared with 3.6 for AMX and FujiFilm Type II. PEI-Et, PEI-Bu-Me, and PEI-Mix also had lower water transport numbers than the commercial benchmarks. Limiting water transfer helps concentrate salt without bringing as much water into the receiving stream.

The measured electrical power consumption included substantial contributions from the cation-exchange membrane and solution channels. PEI membranes were broadly competitive with commercial materials; their strongest improvements were in the combination of current efficiency, ion discrimination, and water transport.

Why brine selectivity differs from dilute-solution selectivity

The chloride/sulfate coefficients increased markedly in the concentrated brine experiment. Supporting measurements showed that equilibration in 1 M NaCl reduced water uptake by 21%, 24%, and 21% for PEI-Pr-Me, PEI-Bu-Me, and PEI-Mix, respectively. The supplementary table gives a 12% reduction for PEI-Me.

The paper links this stronger dehydration of membranes with mixed alkyl groups to narrower hydrated transport domains and greater discrimination against sulfate. This provides a plausible explanation for the increased chloride preference at high ionic strength. The practical choice also depends on the intended product: retaining sulfate is useful for a chloride-rich concentrate, while greater sulfate transport may be useful when sulfate accumulation in the diluate is undesirable.

What remains to be tested?

The synthetic brine contained chloride and sulfate, without calcium, magnesium, or natural organic matter. The paper identifies removal of scale-forming cations as a pretreatment consideration and the influence of natural organic matter on anion transport as a direction for further work.
