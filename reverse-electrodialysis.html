Research notes / Daniil V. Golubenko

Ion-exchange membranes for reverse electrodialysis

← Research interests · All publications

Turning a salinity gradient into electricity

Reverse electrodialysis (RED) generates electricity as ions move between concentrated and dilute solutions through alternating cation-exchange and anion-exchange membranes. The membranes must conduct the desired ions while limiting salt leakage and water transport, which consume the salinity gradient without producing useful current.

In this study, we prepared membranes by radiation-induced grafting of functionalized polystyrene onto polymethylpentene (PMP) films and compared them with commercial FujiFilm and RALEX membranes. With 0.1/1 M NaCl feeds, the more selective GCM-2/GAM-2 pair achieved a maximum net power density of 0.67 W/m² and a calculated current efficiency of 88%. With 0.1/5 M NaCl, the less selective GCM-1/GAM-1 pair produced the highest net power density, 2.1 W/m², but its calculated current efficiency fell to 14%.

These two results reveal a central design tradeoff: a membrane can deliver a high instantaneous power output while allowing substantial salt transport that does not contribute to electrical current. The broader RED context is discussed in the review by Tufa and colleagues cited in our paper.

How grafting and crosslinking changed transport

A 50 μm PMP support was activated by UV irradiation before grafting styrene with divinylbenzene as a crosslinker. Sulfonation produced the cation-exchange membranes (GCM); chloromethylation and quaternization produced the anion-exchange membranes (GAM). Type 1 used a higher grafting degree (112%) and lower crosslinking degree (2.5%); type 2 combined a lower grafting degree (47%) with higher crosslinking (10%). Related preparation methods are described in our earlier papers on cation-exchange membranes and anion-exchange membranes.

Ion-exchange capacity (IEC) refers to dry membrane mass. Water uptake was measured in distilled water; resistance and salt permeability were measured using 0.5 M NaCl, with conductivity measurements at 25 °C. Apparent permselectivity was obtained from membrane potentials across a 0.1/0.5 M NaCl gradient.

Type 1 contained more exchange sites per gram and absorbed more water, providing lower electrical resistance. Type 2 restricted swelling and raised the concentration of fixed charges in the absorbed water. This strengthened ion exclusion and greatly reduced salt leakage: GCM-2 had approximately 36 times lower NaCl permeability than GCM-1, while GAM-2 had approximately 10 times lower permeability than GAM-1. The cost was higher membrane resistance. This competition between hydration, conductivity, and selectivity is also discussed in the cited studies by Fan and Yip and Russell and colleagues.

What the laboratory stack measured

The stack contained four cation/anion membrane pairs, each with an active area of 9 cm², giving a total tested membrane area of 72 cm². Solution channels used 1 mm gaskets and nonconductive mesh spacers. The dilute feed was 0.1 M NaCl; the concentrated feed was either 1 or 5 M NaCl. Feed velocities were 0.28, 0.74, and 1.1 cm/s. The relatively concentrated dilute feed helped reduce the resistance of these thick solution channels.

Power density was normalized to the total active area of both cation- and anion-exchange membranes. Electrode contributions were corrected using a blank experiment, illustrated in the supplementary material. Net power density additionally subtracts hydraulic pumping losses calculated from the feed flow rates and measured pressure drops. This net value describes the tested stack and its feed pumping.

The reported current efficiency is the calculated fraction of total salt transport associated with current-carrying ion migration. The remaining fraction represents nonselective salt diffusion. Overall conversion of the available mixing energy also depends on how much of the gradient is used, water transport, and operating conditions.

At 0.1/1 M: selectivity outweighed the lowest membrane resistance

GCM-2/GAM-2 produced about 18% more net power than FujiFilm Type I in the same stack and retained a much higher calculated current efficiency (88% versus 57%). Although GCM-1/GAM-1 had the lowest membrane resistance, it produced only 0.47 W/m². In this stack, solution-channel resistance dominated; the type 1 membranes contributed only about 5–6% of the total resistance. Further lowering their resistance therefore offered little compensation for increased salt leakage and reduced voltage.

The result makes membrane selectivity particularly valuable when the electrolyte channels already control most of the electrical losses. A membrane property measured in isolation must therefore be interpreted together with the stack geometry and feed conductivity.

At 0.1/5 M: higher peak power came with greater gradient loss

The stronger gradient increased the available driving force, but also intensified salt diffusion and osmosis. GCM-1/GAM-1 reached 2.1 W/m² compared with 1.8 W/m² for GCM-2/GAM-2. Their calculated current efficiencies were 14% and 53%, respectively: most salt transport through the type 1 pair was diffusive.

Salt leakage can increase the concentration of the dilute solution and reduce its electrical resistance. The paper's calculation at a feed velocity of 1 cm/s predicts that the dilute stream could rise from 0.10 to about 0.16 M NaCl across a stack using type 1 membranes. This helps explain the high peak power, while the same leakage consumes the salinity difference. More selective membranes can consequently be preferable when conserving a finite supply of concentrated and dilute solutions is important, including recirculating operation. The distinction between power output and use of the available mixing energy is developed in the cited exergy analysis by Giacalone and colleagues.

Why selectivity and voltage depend on the working gradient

The pair-averaged apparent permselectivity of GCM-1/GAM-1 decreased from 90.6% across 0.1/0.5 M NaCl to 82.8% across 0.1/1 M and 59.1% across 0.1/5 M. GCM-2/GAM-2 retained 95.7%, 90.8%, and 77.3%, respectively. Increasing salt concentration weakens exclusion of unwanted ions, making selectivity measured at a moderate gradient an incomplete predictor of operation with concentrated brine.

Diffusion and osmosis also change the concentrations near the membranes and along the channels. The supplementary analysis connects larger calculated diffusive salt fluxes with a larger shortfall in stack open-circuit voltage relative to that expected from membrane-potential measurements. Faster flow reduces these concentration changes and can raise voltage, but requires additional pumping energy. The supplementary gross-power and pumping-loss curves show why increasing flow does not give an unlimited increase in net power; the paper places typical net-power optima around 0.5–1 cm/s.

These effects connect material properties with concentration polarization and channel design. Relevant sources cited in the paper include Gurreri and colleagues on concentration polarization and Galama and colleagues on resistance under salinity gradients.

What the calculations explain

The stack calculations used measured membrane transport properties to estimate voltage, resistance, power, and the balance between migration and salt diffusion. They reproduced the performance trends more closely at 0.1/1 M NaCl. At 0.1/5 M, the deviations became larger as diffusion and water transport altered the working gradient. For example, the calculated net power for GCM-1/GAM-1 was 1.5 W/m², compared with the measured 2.1 W/m²; for GCM-2/GAM-2, the corresponding values were 2.2 and 1.8 W/m².

The model did not explicitly resolve nonohmic concentration-polarization losses or concentration changes along the solution channels. Those simplifications matter most for highly permeable membranes and strong gradients. The modelling framework builds on the RED analysis by Güler and colleagues cited in the manuscript.

What remains to be tested?

The comparisons were made in a small laboratory stack with NaCl feeds and 1 mm channels. Testing other channel geometries and operating conditions would establish how the balance between membrane and solution resistance changes. The paper also identifies the need to account more fully for concentration changes and polarization when predicting performance at strong salinity gradients.
