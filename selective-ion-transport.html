Research / Selective ion transport / 2024

PBI-modified Nafion for monovalent-ion-selective electrodialysis

← Research interests · All publications

We introduced polybenzimidazole (PBI) into one side of a Nafion-117 cation-exchange membrane to favour monovalent ions over divalent ions during electrodialysis. Across the tested operating conditions, the selected membrane reached selectivity coefficients of about 27 for Na+/Mg2+, 11 for Li+/Mg2+, and 3.8 for Na+/Ca2+. These are pair-specific maxima across different experiments, rather than performance values guaranteed at one common operating point.

Research by Daniil Golubenko, Dmitrii Petukhov, Raed A. Al-Juboori, Daniel Johnson, and Nidal Hilal at New York University Abu Dhabi. Published in ACS Applied Polymer Materials (2024). Article and Supplementary Materials.

Membrane design · Properties · Separation · Mechanism · Limitations

Why separate alkali and alkaline-earth ions?

A conventional cation-exchange membrane distinguishes cations from anions but does not necessarily separate lithium, sodium, or potassium from magnesium, calcium, and strontium. Greater control within the cation group is relevant to mineral recovery and selective desalination. In this study, the target was a membrane that retained cation conduction while reducing the passage of divalent ions.

Related approaches cited in the paper include layer-by-layer modification for controlling ion selectivity, by Abdu et al. (2014), and polyelectrolyte multilayers on Nafion, by White et al. (2016). Their materials and operating protocols differ, so a comparison requires more than matching the largest selectivity number.

How was PBI introduced into Nafion?

One face of a pretreated Nafion-117 membrane in the proton form was contacted with 4 wt% benzenetetramine tetrahydrochloride in 0.1 M HCl. Contact times of 1, 3, 6, and 60 minutes were compared. After rinsing, that face was exposed to 2 wt% terephthalaldehyde in acetone for two hours, followed by washing and conditioning.

The six-minute precursor treatment produced the sample referred to as PBI-6m, also labelled PBI-C6-6m in the property table. Six minutes describes the first treatment step, not the total fabrication time. The one-sided approach aimed to introduce a selective region while limiting the resistance cost of modifying the entire membrane.

Raman mapping detected the PBI-associated signal extending approximately 50–70 μm from the treated face, with intensity decreasing through the thickness. This supports a graded modified region within the host membrane, rather than a uniformly modified bulk material.

How PBI changes hydration and ion transport

PBI changes the environment through which ions move. Its basic nitrogen groups can interact with Nafion's acidic sulfonic groups, forming an interpolymer complex. The paper interprets this ionic pairing as reducing the population of exchangeable counterions and the water associated with them. Lower hydration, in turn, restricts the space available for ion transport.

For the selected PBI-6m membrane, water uptake decreased from 24.0% to 15.7%, while ion-exchange capacity decreased from 0.95 to 0.66 mmol g−1. Together, these changes support the picture of a less hydrated transport environment with fewer accessible exchange sites. They help explain why introducing PBI lowers both ionic conductivity and salt diffusion.

The most abrupt change occurred between the one- and three-minute precursor treatments: sodium-form conductivity dropped from 3.58 to 0.019 mS cm−1. The paper associates this transition with the formation of a more complete selective region. Increasing treatment to 60 minutes produced further reductions in hydration and conductivity, while six minutes gave the highest selectivity in the screening experiments. Longer treatment therefore did not automatically improve separation.

At the same time, the apparent cation transport number rose from 92.2% to 97.4% for PBI-6m. The proposed explanation is that reduced hydration leaves less electroneutral solution within the membrane and strengthens co-ion exclusion. This measurement describes discrimination between cations and anions; the separation between different cations is established by the mixed-salt electrodialysis experiments below.

The diffusion measurements show how strongly the modified environment restricts salt passage. NaCl permeability decreased from 5.26 × 10−8 to 1.3 × 10−9 cm2 s−1, approximately a 40-fold reduction. MgCl2 permeability decreased from 1.2 × 10−7 to 2.9 × 10−11 cm2 s−1, more than 4,000-fold. The much stronger suppression of magnesium-salt diffusion supports the observed preference for monovalent ions.

These observations identify the central trade-off: a tighter, less hydrated selective region discriminates more strongly between ions, but also makes conduction more difficult. The material-property measurements, structural evidence, and electrodialysis results together explain the membrane's behaviour.

Conductivity was measured in the sodium form by two-electrode impedance in deionized water at 20–22 °C. Apparent transport numbers came from potentials across 0.1 and 0.5 M NaCl, with junction-potential corrections. Diffusion permeability was measured without an applied electric field.

Ionic clusters and transport channels

Combining SAXS with hydration data and the Gierke model gave average ionic-cluster diameters of 4.2 nm for Nafion and 3.7 nm for PBI-6m. These are model-based estimates averaged through the membrane thickness. They do not directly measure the narrow connecting transport channels. The underlying morphology model is discussed in Gierke et al. (1981), cited in the study.

Independent diffusion tests with methanol, ethylene glycol, glycerol, and glucose supported a reduction in accessible channel size. Glycerol and glucose transport through PBI-6m fell below detection limits. From the molecular-size dependence, the paper estimated a channel radius of approximately 2.0–2.5 Å. This is an indirect estimate, distinct from the nanometre-scale cluster diameter.

How was selective electrodialysis tested?

The feed contained LiCl, NaCl, KCl, MgCl2, CaCl2, and SrCl2, each at 3, 6, or 9 mM. Tests covered 0.4–3.0 mA cm−2. The modified face pointed toward the diluate. Active membrane area was 5.32 cm2, with a 1 L feed reservoir and a 25 or 50 mL concentrate reservoir. The concentrate initially contained 10 mM NH4Cl; the supporting and electrode solutions were 30 mM NH4Cl and 0.1 M Na2SO4, respectively.

Solutions circulated at 160 mL min−1. Test durations were 200, 160, 80, and 53 minutes at 0.4, 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 mA cm−2, respectively. Concentrate samples were analysed by ion chromatography, and concentration-versus-time slopes were used to estimate ion fluxes.

The selectivity coefficient P(A/B) is the A-to-B flux ratio normalized by their feed concentrations. For an equimolar mixture, it reduces to the flux ratio. It is neither a recovery percentage nor a product-purity measurement. The paper estimates 10–15% uncertainty in these coefficients.

Current and concentration change the outcome

At 3 mM of each salt, PBI-6m showed a Na+/Mg2+ coefficient of about 17 at 0.4 mA cm−2, falling to about 3 at 3.0 mA cm−2. Thus, raising current increased the throughput demand but weakened discrimination between sodium and magnesium.

At a fixed 2 mA cm−2, raising each salt concentration from 3 to 9 mM increased the sodium-over-magnesium and sodium-over-strontium coefficients by roughly four- to five-fold. Calcium and lithium did not follow the same simple monotonic pattern. This makes a single “selectivity of the membrane” an incomplete description without the feed and current.

Current efficiency associated with the measured cation transport was about 80–90% in the main discussion; the Supplementary Materials describe it as remaining close to 90% for the modified membrane. High current efficiency does not imply high lithium purity or lithium recovery.

Equilibration and repeated testing

A stability series at 0.4 mA cm−2 involved 200-minute cycles, with an overnight pause between cycles three and four. The manuscript describes a total testing period of 20 hours. Monovalent-ion fluxes settled comparatively quickly, while divalent-ion fluxes approached equilibrium after approximately 17 hours, including the overnight period with the membrane in the cell.

The same PBI-6m sample accumulated roughly 150–200 hours across different tests, including trial runs, polarization curves, and impedance measurements. That history supports continued function during laboratory testing, but is not a continuous industrial service-life measurement.

Why does PBI modification suppress divalent-ion transport?

The proposed explanation combines a less hydrated membrane structure with restricted mobility in the modified region. PBI can interact with Nafion's sulfonic groups, reducing mobile counterion content and hydration. Narrower pathways make partial dehydration and movement between charged sites more consequential, especially for divalent ions.

At the same time, current–voltage curves showed that divalent-ion conduction increased as the applied voltage increased. The paper therefore discusses ionic blockage as a possible transport mechanism: an ion faces a significant barrier to movement within a narrow charged pathway, and a stronger electric field facilitates crossing that barrier.

The relevant context includes Xie et al. (2023), ionic blockade in angstrom-scale channels, and Kavokine et al. (2019), ionic Coulomb blockade and the Wien effect. These cited studies motivate the interpretation; they do not directly establish the microscopic structure of the PBI/Nafion composite.

What did polarization and impedance reveal?

The experiments compared individual NaCl and MgCl2 solutions with mixed electrolytes. For the modified membrane in magnesium chloride, impedance showed two overlapping processes whose resistances decreased with increasing voltage. This was consistent with easier divalent-ion transport under stronger driving conditions.

In mixed feeds, concentration polarization can increase the resistance associated with sodium transport while voltage facilitates magnesium transport, reducing Na/Mg selectivity. Interpreting each ion's flux relative to a calculated limiting flux requires caution because the calculation's transport-number assumptions can be inaccurate for divalent ions in the modified membrane.

The Supplementary Materials also show that low-voltage impedance measurements depend strongly on equilibration: extending conditioning from 5 to 60 minutes reduced the non-equilibrium tail in the Nyquist plot. This is another reason to separate transient measurements from steady transport behaviour.

Operating considerations and future directions

  • Current and equilibration. Higher current reduced monovalent/divalent discrimination, and divalent-ion transport took many hours to equilibrate. Both effects matter when choosing conditions and interpreting selectivity.
  • Energy evaluation. The paper notes that laboratory-cell solution resistance differs from an industrial stack. Application-specific testing is needed to estimate process energy consumption.
  • Material design and modelling. The proposed next steps are to vary PBI chemistry and the host membrane, and to use modelling to clarify ion transport and identify suitable separation conditions.
